The Conversation with my Manager

How you tell your manager you're leaving for Fulbright, especially if you like your job and manager?

I was certainly terrified of telling my manager I was pursuing Fulbright, especially after only half a year of working there. My mind was constantly thinking about how to tell my manager in a way that they will allow me to return to the team in the future. I thought about how this Fulbright thing would be so short-lived compared to the rest of my life, and all I could hope for was a swift job search upon completion, hopefully with the same team. This is of course not guaranteed, I realized that many of the factors that go into whether you can return or not are out of your control. In aerospace, for example, it's common for hiring rates to be dependent on the company winning contracts. On your specific team, they might hire someone else quickly who can do the job after you left. Or maybe they didn’t like you and they’re happy you’re leaving. Whatever it is, much of your ability to return is out of your control, so I found that thinking about the best way to convince management to stay was fruitless. That said, there are a lot of ways you can screw it up and leave a bad impression, turning an opportunity you had to one that slips out of your fingers. So there are some things you can do to give yourself the best chance.

First, how far in advance? My advice would be 1.5 to 2 months. Training for engineering roles are quite an investment from the company and team, and while a two-week notice is the “rule of thumb”, giving two-weeks notice in an engineering environment is often too soon. You want to be as respectful to your manager as possible; remember, you still need a job when you get back from Fulbright. That said, I would say there is a “too early”. If you tell your manager too far in advance, then your impending departure will linger, and you will ultimately just be “the guy who is leaving soon” in your office. This could cause fellow engineers to loop you less into meetings, and you may find yourself with gradually diminishing responsibility before you expected it. I told my manager just under 2 months before I was leaving, and by the time I had 1 month left, I had begun to notice that I wasn’t getting new responsibilities. I was more or less just chugging along with what I had.

Finally, the timing is right. You sit in the room with your manager. Maybe your eyes lock and your manager hits you with the "so how's your work coming along?" Or maybe you exchange greetings and sit there while they wait for you to make the first move. No matter the level of tension in the room, you just have to say the words. Say them as if you are in a room by yourself with nobody to hear, and let the world react to you. For me, it was something along the lines of: “I would like to bring something important to your attention. I was recently awarded an opportunity to conduct research abroad through a Fulbright grant. Thus, I will be unfortunately leaving Honeywell in [x timeframe]. “ I then baused a bit to let my manager respond. I didn't want to spill out my entire script at once. This is because at this point, a bunch of things will rush to the manager's mind, and they're gonna want to start talking or expressing their feelings through body language. So by giving them the opportunity to say or act these things, they will feel heard. Once this happens, I think it's also important to elevate the value of the work experience you have, so I said: “It was a really difficult decision, as I’ve enjoyed both the projects I’ve worked on as well as the team I’ve gotten to collaborate with. I’ve learned so much and have really enjoyed contributing to your team under your leadership.” I also reassured my manager that I would continue working on the projects I had until the end of my time with the company, and that I would take responsibility for helping onboard and train the next new hire while handing off the work smoothly. This included documentation and read-me's to make things as clear as possible.

There was a question I got that I wasn't really prepared to be asked. My manager asked specifically why I was pursuing Fulbright. I had that answer memorized while writing my application, but I wasn't ready for it here. I answered with something along the lines of: "While the skills are important to me, the travel and cultural connections fostered through the Fulbright program are particularly attractive to me. This opportunity for me to pursue international experiences has been a dream of mine for a long time, and getting to do it while building the technical skills simultaneously makes it a great fit for me. Again, it was a difficult decision, as I’ve enjoyed the work here as well."

The last thing I wanted to do was to undermine the experience I had while working at Honeywell. So I did some thinking about the ways I could mess this up as well. Here were some things that came to mind for me: 1. Don’t try to convince your manager that Fulbright is “better”, or that you’re superior because you got Fulbright. Different paths are better for different people, and you’re simply on your own path. This may seem obvious, but it’s easy to slip up in certain ways that may come off as such, especially if your manager tries to convince you to stay: I’ll be building better skills for myself”, “The company would never give me this opportunity”, “I just need a different environment”, “[City of Fulbright location] is so much better than [City I’m currently working in]”. You can have your own opinions. But you’re trying to keep options open here, so be humble. Remember, you're not pursuing a better opportunity, you’re pursuing a different opportunity. 2. Don’t ask your manager whether they will let you return. It’s easy to want to work this into the conversation, because everybody likes a sense of security. But let’s face it: if you were a manager, would you really be okay with having a vacant position open on your team for almost a year, knowing that the rest of your team has to work overtime to make up for you? Now it’s not unheard of; some managers will literally tell you to reach back out when you return to the US. But don’t expect it. You can instead share your enthusiasm about keeping in touch during the job-seeking process, in case there’s an opening you can apply for (again, don’t assume your manager will just give you the position). 3. Don’t ask your manager whether you should go or not. Again, it sounds obvious. But when you’re unsure of what kind of impact Fulbright is going to have on your career, it’s easy to seek advice from the guy who hired you. But keep in mind that he has a job and a boss too. His job is to get good talent employed on his team, and help his team achieve their personal goals while they make money for the company. Leaving the decision to your manager is one of the worst things you can do, and there are few options that come out of it. One, they hold it over your head and tell you to stay, because if you leave then you won’t come back. Two, you look like you don’t have a strong vision for yourself. You ultimately have to make the hard decision.
