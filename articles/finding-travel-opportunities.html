Finding Travel Opportunities

Many people dream of travelling the world, but it can be hard to find opportunities that align professionally.
Especially as an engineering student, where it seems like you're not the target audience for these opportunities.

I had spent lots of time dreaming about travelling the world early in college. I watched others do it and I wanted to find those opportunities myself. But only 20% of Fulbright study/research applicants receive grants, and the most competitive applicants can prove their adaptability to foreign environments. They're able to use their previous experiences to convince the panel that "I can conquer this and even more too, because I conquered that."

One day during my first semester of four-year school, I had taken a rather unorthodox route to get to class. The door outside the building had faded paint and dents, and it was the kind of door where you didn’t know if it would take you to class or to another dimension. But I had time to kill, so I did the slow awkward walk up to the door, glancing around in case someone was sitting in the bush to notify me that I was trespassing.

It led to a stairwell. A rather uninspiring one. Bare, gray concrete. It was chilly inside, and while slowly creeping up the stairs, I came across a bulletin board with a few scattered flyers on it. Some of them had torn pages, some had scribbled handwriting. But one of them was a call for applications. So I did, in the manner of "I don't have a chance at this, but I'll see what happens." And soon enough, I had myself a summer research internship in Germany.

Two years later, I found a partially-folded sheet of paper, laying on the ground with footprints on it. On that paper was a QR code to RSVP for an info session that led me to a summer internship in South Korea.

I see myself as ridiculously lucky, as there are so few golden tickets like the ones I found laying around campus. But the truth is, bulletin boards are flooded with flyers, and it isn't uncommon to see a flyer that send engineering students out to see the real world, in places you would least expect, tucked behind some club callouts. Often times, you won't even find these opportunities in engineering buildings; you might have to go into places you’ve never explored.

You may know of the analogy where you do not know how many red cars you saw today, but you could count easily if you started paying attention. Or the moment you learn about something cool, you start to see it pop up everywhere.

1: Constantly be on the lookout for opportunities. You might find them where you least expect.

To this day, I still tell the same old stories about my internship in Germany to the new people I meet. My family and friends can't count on both hands the number of times they've heard about the time I watched Germany's Olympic luge team lay in their sled, in the wind tunnel where I interned. Now imagine the yapping when I came back for the fall semester. It was the only thing on my mind. You might have found find my physical presence, but mentally I was off exploring every nook and cranny of our world. I built an obsession for street view on Google Maps, and I still spend far too much time clicking on blue-colored streets in the most remote of locations, solely to scratch the itch in my brain of "What else could possibly be out there?"

Maybe I was annoying about it to most. Maybe to others, "annoying" isn't a strong enough word. But at least I excited one faculty member, who was so fascinated by the travel bug in me that they referred me to a two-week study abroad program in Mexico to take a course on supply chain management. Yet another opportunity for me to leave my comfort zone and explore a new culture.

Sharing these ambitions with professors and academic advisors allowed them to connect me with opportunities I wouldn’t know exist otherwise.

2: Let faculty and academic advisors know your goals. Tell them exactly what you want to do. Tell them your wildest dreams about travelling. And be really, really passionate about it.

But to build these exciting experiences for yourself, you don’t even have to be in some distant land. Literally anything that can take you out of town is a great opportunity.

I was fortunate enough to spend most of my undergrad experience working on a team that built weather balloon payloads. It ended up being my senior project. Now depending on who you talk to, this may not be as glamorous as building the next reusable rocket, or building an autonomous RC aircraft that flies perfectly between cones in a field. But it gave me opportunities to travel. We would set our alarms for 4am on a Saturday, load up the old trucks with the university logo faded on the side, and pray that our 3D-printed payloads would just stay together on the drive into northern Nevada. After launching, we might stop at McDonald's for food, but more importantly, a place for us to track down the weather balloon we just launched, hoping that it would land remotely close to a road we predicted it would land by the night before. It was always a mystery. Would you be able to see it from the road? Would you have to drive through a wash to get to it? Would you have to hike up that mountain over there? And who would fall asleep first on the drive back? (The answer was usually everyone simultaneously, minus the driver). Our team even flew to Arkansas one time to launch our weather balloon during the total solar eclipse (which is a story that I’ve told to people way too much at this point).

During my graduate studies, I joined an outing club that would organize hiking and other outdoor trips. I also sporadically participated in the astronomy club during my undergrad, when I knew they would be taking their telescopes away from the city to avoid the light pollution. I was looking for literally anything to get me travelling somewhere. These opportunities also exist in rocket clubs and FSAE teams, but you have to get creative with it. This sort of circles back to the first tip about keeping your eyes open, but I mean keeping your eyes open for anything.

3: Find field research opportunities. Find clubs that go out and explore. Find a group of friends who also enjoy exploration. Seek out and surround yourself with those who go out in the field for their work. Soon enough, that will be you.

And finally, something that can really be applied to lots of things in life. Some of my most formative experiences were ones where I simply traveled to a conference in another state. I met so many fascinating people during those times, and even going to a new state I had never been to was an incredible amount of fun. It didn’t even matter where I went; what mattered more is that I always felt good after trying something new.

4: Be the first to raise your hand to go. Take every exploration opportunity you can. No matter how big or small. Every opportunity is a good one.
