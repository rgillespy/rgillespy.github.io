Finding Travel Opportunities as an Engineering Student

Many people dream of travelling the world.
But it can be hard to find opportunities that align professionally as an engineering student.

One day during my first semester of four-year school, I had taken a rather unorthodox route to get to class. The door outside the building had faded paint and dents, and it was the kind of door where you didn’t know if it would take you to class or to another dimension. But I had time to kill, so I did the slow awkward walk up to the door, glancing around in case someone was sitting in the bush to notify me that I was trespassing.

It led to a stairwell. Rather uninspiring. Bare, gray concrete. A little chilly inside. When I slowly crept up the stairs, I came across a bulletin board with a few scattered flyers on it. Some of them had torn pages, some had scribbled handwriting. But one of them was a call for applications. After standing for a while, staring at the words at the paper, I sent out an application. It was in the manner of "I don't have a chance at this, but I'll see what happens." Soon enough, I had myself a summer research internship in Germany.

Two years later, I found a partially-folded sheet of paper, laying on the ground with footprints on it. On that paper was a QR code to RSVP for an info session that led me to a summer internship in South Korea.

I was probably quite lucky, as there are so few golden tickets like the ones I found laying around campus. But the truth is, bulletin boards are flooded with flyers, and it isn't uncommon to see a flyer that send engineering students out to see the real world, in places you would least expect, tucked behind some club callouts. Often times, you won't even find these opportunities in engineering buildings; you might have to go into places you’ve never explored.

You may know of the analogy where you do not know how many red cars you saw today, but you could count easily tomorrow if I told you to pay attention. Or you start to see a new trend pop up everywhere, only after you learned about it. I see travel opportunities the same way.

1: Constantly look for opportunities. You might find them where you least expect.

To this day, I still tell the same old stories about my internship in Germany to the new people I meet. My family and friends can't count on both hands the number of times they've heard about the time I watched Germany's Olympic luge team lay in their sled, in the wind tunnel where I interned. Now imagine the yapping when I came back for the fall semester. It was the only thing on my mind. You might have found find my physical presence, but mentally I was off exploring every nook and cranny of our world. I built an obsession for street view on Google Maps, and I still spend far too much time clicking on blue-colored streets in the most remote of locations, solely to scratch the itch in my brain of "What else could possibly be out there?"

Maybe I was annoying about it to most. Maybe to others, "annoying" isn't a strong enough descriptor. But at least I excited one faculty member, who was so fascinated by the travel bug in me that they referred me to a two-week study abroad program in Mexico to take a course on supply chain management. It was yet another opportunity for me to leave my comfort zone and explore a new culture.

Sharing these ambitions with professors and academic advisors allowed them to connect me with opportunities I wouldn’t know exist otherwise.

2: Let faculty and academic advisors know your goals. Tell them exactly what you want to do. Tell them your wildest dreams about travelling. And be really, really passionate about it.

But to build these exciting experiences for yourself, you don’t have to be in some distant land. Literally any train that can take you out of your town of comfort is a great opportunity.

I was fortunate enough to spend most of my undergrad experience working on a team that built weather balloon payloads. It ended up being the greatest senior project I could ask for. We would set our alarms for 4am on a Saturday, load up the old trucks with the university logo faded on the side, and enjoy the sunrise while on the road praying that our 3D-printed payloads would just stay together on the drive into northern Nevada. After launching, we might stop at McDonald's for food, but more importantly, a place for us to track down the weather balloon we just launched, hoping we would spot it remotely close to our predicted landing spot from the night before. It was always a mystery. Could you see it from the road? Would you have to drive through a wash to get to it? Maybe hike up and over that daunting over there? Who would fall asleep first on the drive back? And why is it always me?

During my graduate studies, I regularly took day trips on the weekends out in to nature, and sometimes overnight camping, with the school's outing club. I also sporadically participated in the astronomy club during my undergrad, when I knew they would be taking their telescopes away from the city to avoid the light pollution. I was looking for literally anything to get me travelling somewhere. These opportunities also exist in rocket clubs and FSAE teams, so get creative with it. This circles back to the first tip about keeping your eyes open, but I mean keeping your eyes open for anything.

3: Find field research opportunities. Find clubs that go out and explore. Find a group of friends who also enjoy exploration. Seek out and surround yourself with those who go out in the field. Soon enough, that will be you.

Although I had vivid dreams of finding myself on the other side of the world, some of my most formative experiences were ones where I simply traveled to a conference in another state. Part of it was the rush of meeting a hundred intellects, all as accomplished as you hope to be, ambitious to scribble down their ideas as they speak in their presentations, hoping that half the info doesn't crawls out of your brain after you go to sleep. Even going to a new state I had never been to was an incredible amount of fun. It didn’t matter where; I always felt an incredible joy after looking back on new experiences. Every time there was an opportunity for me to go travel some place new, I was always the first to say "yes". Then I worried about the logistics later. To be honest, life is quite fun that way.

4: Be the first to raise your hand to go. Take every exploration opportunity you can. No matter how big or small. Every opportunity is a good one.
